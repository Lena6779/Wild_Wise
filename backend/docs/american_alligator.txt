American Alligator

Sources: This fact sheet was summarized from https://www.nps.gov/ever/learn/nature/alligator.htm (National Park Service, Everglades National Park) and https://nwf.org/Educational-Resources/Wildlife-Guide/Reptiles/American-Alligator (National Wildlife Federation).

Animal class: The American alligator is a cold-blooded reptile.

Birth: American alligators lay eggs. A female American alligator builds a nest above the water level and can lay up to 90 eggs, which hatch after about 58 to 63 days. The nest temperature decides whether American alligator babies are male or female: warmer nests of 90 to 93 degrees Fahrenheit hatch males, and cooler nests of 82 to 86 degrees hatch females. The mother carries her babies to the water when they call to her.

Diet: American alligators are carnivores. Young American alligators eat insects, small fish, and frogs, while adults eat fish, turtles, small mammals, birds, and other reptiles.

Habitat: American alligators live throughout the southeastern United States, mostly in freshwater swamps and marshes, and also in rivers and lakes. American alligators can spend short periods in salty water around mangroves.

Hibernation or migration: American alligators become inactive, or dormant, when the weather turns cold. American alligators stop feeding when the water drops below about 68 to 73 degrees Fahrenheit, and they dig dens and tunnels to shelter from cold or very dry weather.

Fun facts: Adult male American alligators can grow 13 to 15 feet long, and females reach about 10 feet. The holes American alligators dig in the dry season hold water that helps other animals survive. American alligators were listed as endangered in 1967 and recovered enough to be removed from the list in 1987.
