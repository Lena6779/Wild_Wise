Trapdoor Spider

Sources: This fact sheet was summarized from https://fieldofmar-e.schools.nsw.gov.au/fact-sheets/invertebrates/trapdoor-spiders-fact-sheet (Field of Mars Environmental Education Centre, New South Wales Department of Education).

Animal class: The trapdoor spider is an arachnid, not an insect. Trapdoor spiders are related to tarantulas and funnel-web spiders, and the name covers several families of spiders.

Birth: Trapdoor spiders lay eggs. A female trapdoor spider lays her eggs inside her burrow, where they hatch. Baby trapdoor spiders stay in the burrow for a while, then spread out and dig small burrows of their own.

Diet: Trapdoor spiders are carnivores that eat animals living on the ground, such as beetles, crickets, cockroaches, slaters, spiders, and moths. Trapdoor spiders do not build webs to catch food. A trapdoor spider waits near the entrance of its burrow and rushes out to grab prey that comes close.

Habitat: Trapdoor spiders live in burrows lined with silk, dug into soil in lawns, gardens, banks, and bushland. Some trapdoor spiders cover their burrows with a hinged door made of soil, silk, and plants, while many others leave the burrow open with a silk rim.

Hibernation or migration: The source for this fact sheet does not say whether trapdoor spiders hibernate or migrate. Female trapdoor spiders usually stay in or near their burrows for most of their lives and make the burrow wider as they grow. Adult male trapdoor spiders leave their burrows to search for females.

Fun facts: Some trapdoor spiders live 5 to 20 years. The oldest spider ever recorded was a female trapdoor spider from Western Australia called Number 16, who lived to at least 43 years old. A trapdoor spider's fangs move downward instead of side to side.
